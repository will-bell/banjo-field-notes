# Double C chord chart

Tuning: `gCGCD`

The four long strings are:

```text
4th  3rd  2nd  1st
 C    G    C    D
```

The short 5th string is `g` and is treated as a drone rather than part of the movable-shape calculation.

A shape like `2-4-2-5` means frets on the 4th, 3rd, 2nd, and 1st strings respectively.

## Quick reference

Useful low-position chords:

| Chord | Shape | Notes on long strings |
|---|---:|---|
| C | `0-0-0-2` | C G C E |
| F | `0-2-0-3` | C A C F |
| G | `2-4-2-5` | D B D G |
| G7 | `2-4-5-5` | D B F G |
| Am | `0-2-0-2` | C A C E |
| Dm | `2-2-2-3` | D A D F |
| Em | `4-4-4-5` | E B E G |

Notice how Double C makes C-family harmony feel very different physically from Open G.

# View 1: I know the chord. Where can I play it?

Double C also gives us three repeating complete major-triad families.

The names here are partly descriptive. Unlike the standard Open G labels “F-shape,” “D-shape,” and “barre,” Double C shape names are not as universally standardized.

I am using:

- **C-shape** — starts with open C as `0-0-0-2`
- **Compact inversion** — the alternating one-fret shape such as `4-5-4-5`
- **F-shape** — starts with low-position F as `0-2-0-3`

These are the practical complete major-triad voicings through fret 12.

| Chord | Shapes (4th → 1st) |
|---|---|
| C | `0-0-0-2` (C-shape) · `4-5-4-5` (Compact inversion) · `7-9-7-10` (F-shape) |
| C# | `1-1-1-3` (C-shape) · `5-6-5-6` (Compact inversion) · `8-10-8-11` (F-shape) |
| D | `2-2-2-4` (C-shape) · `6-7-6-7` (Compact inversion) · `9-11-9-12` (F-shape) |
| Eb | `3-3-3-5` (C-shape) · `7-8-7-8` (Compact inversion) |
| E | `4-4-4-6` (C-shape) · `8-9-8-9` (Compact inversion) |
| F | `0-2-0-3` (F-shape) · `5-5-5-7` (C-shape) · `9-10-9-10` (Compact inversion) |
| F# | `1-3-1-4` (F-shape) · `6-6-6-8` (C-shape) · `10-11-10-11` (Compact inversion) |
| G | `2-4-2-5` (F-shape) · `7-7-7-9` (C-shape) · `11-12-11-12` (Compact inversion) |
| Ab | `0-1-0-1` (Compact inversion) · `3-5-3-6` (F-shape) · `8-8-8-10` (C-shape) |
| A | `1-2-1-2` (Compact inversion) · `4-6-4-7` (F-shape) · `9-9-9-11` (C-shape) |
| Bb | `2-3-2-3` (Compact inversion) · `5-7-5-8` (F-shape) · `10-10-10-12` (C-shape) |
| B | `3-4-3-4` (Compact inversion) · `6-8-6-9` (F-shape) |

If one family disappears near fret 12, it simply continues above the 12th fret.

## Example: C major

```text
C-shape            0-0-0-2    C G C E
Compact inversion  4-5-4-5    E C E G
F-shape            7-9-7-10   G E G C
```

The notes are always C, E, and G. Their order changes.

# Why this is worth learning

Your suspicion is basically right: once the theory becomes internal, “what chord does this shape make here?” becomes less of a lookup problem.

But it is useful during the learning phase because it exposes two separate ideas:

1. **shape identity** — the interval structure under your fingers
2. **chord identity** — which absolute root that structure produces at this fret

Moving a shape one fret does not change its internal interval structure. It transposes the entire chord by one semitone.

That is the bridge between “memorizing banjo grips” and actually understanding movable harmony.

# Comparing Double C with Open G

A useful experiment is to pick one chord — say C major — and compare its three inversions in both tunings.

Open G:

```text
2-0-1-2
5-5-5-5
10-9-8-10
```

Double C:

```text
0-0-0-2
4-5-4-5
7-9-7-10
```

The chord itself has not changed. The tuning changes where its notes happen to live under your fingers.

That is one of the most useful ways to think about alternate tunings.

---

# About the 5th string

The short string is G.

G is:

- the 5th of C
- the root of G
- the minor 7th of A
- not a chord tone in every chord you will play

So, just as in Open G, the drone can either reinforce the harmony or create characteristic banjo tension against it.

For learning the movable shapes, first understand the four long strings. Then listen to what the drone adds.
